### [1] [Cognitive Convergence: Deep Similarities Between Large Language Models and Human Cognition](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.26179)
*Chandra Sripada,Richard Lewis*

Main category: q-bio.NC

TL;DR: Despite fundamental differences in substrate and learning history, LLMs and humans share key cognitive organizing principles across five dimensions (inference, architecture, representation, prediction-driven learning, and reinforcement-like mechanisms), challenging the view of LLMs as purely alien intelligences.


<details>
  <summary>Details</summary>
Motivation: The authors aim to challenge the dominant framing of LLMs as "alien intelligences" whose cognitive operations are fundamentally unlike human cognition, arguing instead that meaningful structural correspondences exist between LLM-based systems and human cognition.

Method: The paper identifies and compares structural correspondences across five dimensions of cognitive organization: inferential organization, computational architecture, representational structure, prediction-driven learning, and reinforcement-learning-like mechanisms supporting goal-directed action.

Result: Contemporary LLM-based systems converge with human cognition on multiple principles of cognitive organization that have longstanding support in cognitive science, despite clear differences in physical substrate, learning history, and environmental interaction.

Conclusion: Core principles long used to explain human intelligence also characterize contemporary LLM-based systems, supporting a broader model of intelligent cognition that bridges human and artificial cognition.

Abstract: LLMs are widely regarded as alien intelligences, systems whose cognitive operations are fundamentally unlike our own. Apparent similarities to human cognition are therefore often seen as the result of anthropomorphic projection. We argue that this framing is mistaken. LLMs clearly differ from humans in important respects, including their physical substrate, learning history, and the environments with which they interact. These differences make it all the more striking that contemporary LLM-based systems converge with human cognition on a number of principles of cognitive organization with longstanding support in cognitive science. We identify structural correspondences across five dimensions: inferential organization, computational architecture, representational structure, prediction-driven learning, and reinforcement-learning-like mechanisms supporting goal-directed action. These correspondences support a broader model of intelligent cognition in which core principles long used to explain human intelligence also characterize contemporary LLM-based systems.

### [2] [Three Failures of Pain Location: Why the Diagnostic Utility of Symptom Localization Is Not One Thing](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.26297)
*Adam Y Shavit*

Main category: q-bio.NC

TL;DR: This paper identifies three distinct ways pain location can be diagnostically misleading—anatomical multiplexing, delocalized amplification, and referred/atypical displacement—unified under a Bayesian inference framework, and argues the traditional "high-accuracy" diagnostic band is overstated.


<details>
  <summary>Details</summary>
Motivation: The prevailing view treats diagnostic utility of pain location as a single gradient based on anatomical complexity, conflating three epistemically distinct failure modes. The authors aim to disentangle these and provide a formal mathematical account.

Method: The paper employs inverse-problem, information-theoretic, decision-theoretic, and neural-field models; introduces a spatial Bayesian model distinguishing reported pain location from felt pain location; and re-examines published diagnostic accuracy data.

Result: Three distinct failure types (multiplexing, amplification, displacement) each have unique mathematical structure and require different remedies. The "high-utility" accuracy band is overstated due to inflated specificity. Observation over time improves recoverable information.

Conclusion: Pain location's diagnostic value is best understood via a "why-location-fails" axis within a Bayesian generative model (likelihood, model class, prior/loss nodes), offering a synthesis distinct from existing nociceptive/neuropathic/nociplastic taxonomies.

Abstract: Patient-reported pain location is diagnostically decisive for some presentations and nearly uninformative for others. The prevailing account treats this as a single gradient of diagnostic utility governed by anatomical complexity. That explanation conflates three epistemically distinct failures of localization, each with its own mathematical structure, optimal instrument, and public-health consequence. In anatomical multiplexing (a), many structures share one location: a non-identifiable inverse problem. In delocalized amplification (b) --- clinically, central sensitization or nociplastic pain --- a centrally driven pain-behaviour pattern replaces the peripheral generator: a change of generative model. In referred/atypical displacement (c), location shifts in a systematic, person-dependent way, as in referred pain and in atypical presentations: a covariate-dependent bias. The three behave differently under inverse-problem, information-theoretic, decision-theoretic, and neural-field models, and demand different remedies. A single principle unifies them: they are one Bayesian inference problem failing at different nodes, meaning different points in one generative model: the likelihood, the model class, and the prior and loss. Observation over time increases the recoverable information. A fourth node, the report itself, carries this paper's main formal contribution --- a spatial Bayesian model of where pain is said to be, distinct from where it is felt. Re-examination further finds that the published "high-utility" accuracy band leans on overstated specificity, so the gradient is real but flatter than drawn. The account sits on a "why-location-fails" axis, a synthesis distinct from the nociceptive/neuropathic/nociplastic taxonomy (Kosek et al., 2016).

</details>


### [3] [Artificial intelligence in deep brain stimulation for movement disorders: a systematic review and technology readiness assessment](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.26666)
*Zohra Souei,Muhammad Mushhood Ur Rehman,Harith Akram,Jocelyne Bloch,Stephan Chabardes,Alfonso Fasano,Marwan Hariz,Joachim K. Krauss,Andrea A. Kühn,Patricia Limousin,Daniel E. Lumsden,Eduardo M. Moraud,Elena Moro,Martin M. Reich,Ludvic Zrinzo,Xavier Vasques,Laura Cif*

Main category: q-bio.NC

TL;DR: This systematic review of 239 DBS-AI studies (2000-2025) finds that while AI methods show promising internal performance for movement disorders, most systems remain at early-to-intermediate translational stages due to limited external validation, small-sample high-dimensional datasets, and biological complexity—though emerging prospective studies signal movement toward clinical maturity.


<details>
  <summary>Details</summary>
Motivation: To systematically characterize the scope, validation maturity, and translational readiness of AI applications in DBS for movement disorders, given the gap between growing research interest and actual clinical deployment.

Method: Systematic evaluation of 239 peer-reviewed studies (2000-2025) assessing AI methods, validation practices, and barriers to clinical translation, including technology readiness assessment.

Result: Research dominated by Parkinson's disease and subthalamic nucleus; internal performance encouraging but external validation rare; evaluations retrospective and single-centre; >1/4 studies had small-sample high-dimensional data with overfitting risk; most systems at early-to-intermediate translational stages; main constraint is limited validation rather than algorithmic inadequacy.

Conclusion: The field is constrained more by validation gaps than algorithmic issues, compounded by biological heterogeneity and dynamic complexity of DBS, but emerging external and prospective studies indicate movement toward clinical maturity with promising applications in targeting, programming, outcome prediction, and adaptive therapy.

Abstract: Artificial intelligence (AI) is increasingly explored across deep brain stimulation (DBS) for movement disorders, yet whether current systems are approaching deployment remains unclear. To characterise their scope, validation maturity, and translational readiness, we systematically evaluated 239 peer-reviewed studies published between 2000 and 2025, assessing AI methods, validation practices, and barriers constraining clinical translation. Research was dominated by Parkinson's disease and subthalamic nucleus targeting, with limited coverage of other disorders and targets. Most studies reported encouraging internal performance; however, external validation was rare, evaluations remained predominantly retrospective and single-centre, and more than one-quarter involved small-sample, high-dimensional datasets with elevated overfitting risk. Technology readiness assessment revealed that most systems remain at early-to-intermediate translational stages, constrained more by limited validation than by algorithmic inadequacy, compounded by the biological heterogeneity and dynamic complexity inherent to DBS. Nevertheless, emerging external and prospective studies suggest a field moving toward clinical maturity, with promising applications in targeting, programming, outcome prediction, and adaptive therapy delivery.

### [4] [Hybrid SINDy-EnKF in Learning Chikungunya Dynamics from Incomplete, Noisy or Partially Observed Data](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.27137)
*Bernard Asamoah Afful,Changhong Mou,Luis Gordillo*

Main category: q-bio.PE

TL;DR: This paper proposes a hybrid data-driven model combining SINDy and Ensemble Kalman Filter to improve Chikungunya virus transmission forecasting and reconstruct unobserved trajectories under noisy, partially observable conditions.


<details>
  <summary>Details</summary>
Motivation: Current mechanistic models for CHIKV rely on uncertain parameters or partial data, limiting their use for forecasting. There is a need for a more robust approach that handles noise and partial observability in epidemiological surveillance.

Method: The authors combine SINDy (for equation discovery) with Ensemble Kalman Filter (for noise filtering and state assimilation). SINDy recovers underlying dynamics; EnKF corrects forecasts from noisy observations and infers unobserved variables.

Result: The hybrid SINDy-EnKF approach improves prediction accuracy and reconstructs unobserved trajectories under partial observability, outperforming standalone SINDy which is highly noise-sensitive.

Conclusion: Embedding SINDy within an EnKF framework provides a robust data-driven modeling tool for epidemic forecasting under realistic noisy and partially observed conditions.

Abstract: Current mechanistic models for the transmission dynamics of the Chikungunya virus (CHIKV) rely on uncertain parameters or partially observed data. This limitation challenges the use of theoretical models for understanding and forecasting disease spread. Here we present a hybrid, data-driven model framework that combines Sparse Identification of Nonlinear Dynamics (SINDy) with the Ensemble Kalman Filter (EnKF) for sequential data assimilation. Our numerical experiments show that this approach improves prediction accuracy and provides a good reconstruction of unobserved trajectories under partial observability, a common constraint in real-world epidemiological surveillance. SINDy can be applied to epidemic trajectories, recovering the underlying equations in noise-free conditions. However, standalone SINDy is highly sensitive to noise, leading to spurious terms and poor performance. Hence, we embed the identification procedure within an EnKF framework, which assimilates noisy observations to correct forecast states from the SINDy-derived model and to infer unobserved state variables.

</details>
