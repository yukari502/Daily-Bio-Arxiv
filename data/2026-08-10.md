### [1] [Control-Anchored Residual Flow Matching Conditioned on Gene Geometry for Virtual Cell Perturbation Modeling](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.06824)
*Quanquan Li,Yihe Chi,Liuyang Song,Hongbo Zhang,Jingyu Li,Xidong Xi,Conghua Wei,Yijie Sun,Yu Chen,Xin Liu,Qi Hu,Jing Ke,Guitao Cao*

Main category: q-bio.MN

TL;DR: GeneGeoFlow is a flow-based model that conditions a control-anchored residual flow on gene-wise geometric embeddings from biological networks (Gene Ontology and coexpression networks) to predict transcriptional responses to unseen perturbations and drug combinations, achieving Pearson Delta scores of 0.8979 on Norman and 0.9088 on ComboSciPlex benchmarks.


<details>
  <summary>Details</summary>
Motivation: Existing graph-based models for virtual cell modeling use the same biological network both to structure gene representations and mediate intergene interactions, conflating stable gene associations with intervention-specific response pathways. Gene Ontology and coexpression networks encode stable relationships rather than perturbation-response directions or magnitudes, so a new structural prior decoupling these is needed.

Method: GeneGeoFlow derives multi-scale spectral coordinates from Gene Ontology and control-derived coexpression networks. A perturbation-conditioned, gene-wise gating module selects relevant structural scales and network sources to yield intervention-specific gene geometry. This geometry conditions a control-anchored residual flow without explicitly propagating target-derived signals along the graph. Training uses condition-wise optimal transport to couple unpaired control and perturbed populations, plus a Delta-correlation objective that aligns predicted and observed condition-level expression-shift directions.

Result: GeneGeoFlow achieves Pearson Delta scores of 0.8979 on the Norman additive benchmark and 0.9088 on five held-out drug combinations in the fixed ComboSciPlex test split.

Conclusion: Perturbation-conditioned gene geometry serves as an effective structural prior for intervention-specific response prediction, avoiding the conflation of stable gene relationships with response propagation.

Abstract: A central task in virtual cell modeling is predicting single-cell transcriptional responses to unseen genetic perturbations and drug combinations, and biological networks provide valuable priors on gene relationships. Existing graph-based models commonly use the same network to structure gene representations and mediate intergene interactions, thereby implicitly treating stable associations as perturbation-response pathways. Gene Ontology and control-derived coexpression networks encode relatively stable relationships rather than intervention-specific response directions or magnitudes. We therefore propose GeneGeoFlow, which conditions a control-anchored residual flow on gene-wise geometry derived from biological networks to learn intervention-specific transcriptional responses. GeneGeoFlow derives multi-scale spectral coordinates from Gene Ontology and control-derived coexpression networks. A perturbation-conditioned, gene-wise gating module selects relevant structural scales and network sources, yielding intervention-specific gene geometry. The resulting geometry conditions a control-anchored residual flow without explicitly propagating target-derived signals along the graph. Condition-wise optimal transport couples unpaired control and perturbed populations for training, while a Delta-correlation objective aligns the predicted and observed condition-level expression-shift directions. GeneGeoFlow achieves Pearson Delta scores of 0.8979 on the Norman additive benchmark and 0.9088 on five held-out drug combinations in the fixed ComboSciPlex test split. These results support perturbation-conditioned gene geometry as an effective structural prior for intervention-specific response prediction, without conflating stable gene relationships with response propagation.

### [2] [Simulating is not always understanding: When model complexity obscures biology](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.06998)
*Lendert Gelens,Alejandro Fábregas-Tejeda,Grant Ramsey,Sylvia Wenmackers,Bart Smeets*

Main category: q-bio.MN

TL;DR: This paper argues that the value of computational models in cell biology depends not on their complexity or biological scope, but on the ratio of free parameters to available experimental constraints and on whether their behavior can be understood. It advocates for simpler, interpretable models with systematic comparison to data over increasingly complex simulations.


<details>
  <summary>Details</summary>
Motivation: Computational models in cell biology span from minimal representations to whole-cell simulations. As models grow more complex, they become harder to interpret, test, and disprove, undermining their contribution to scientific understanding. The field needs a better framework for evaluating what makes models useful.

Method: The paper presents an argumentative analysis, drawing on examples from large-scale agent-based models of cytoskeletal dynamics and tissue mechanics, contrasting their success (built on few physically grounded rules with explorable parameter spaces) against models whose free parameters outpace available experimental constraints.

Result: The paper concludes that the key to understanding is not model size or dimensionality but the parameter-to-constraint ratio and the ability to see why a model produces its behaviors. It proposes a shift toward systematic comparison with simpler models, dynamical analysis, and explicit model hierarchies.

Conclusion: The field should move away from maximizing parameters and instead prioritize interpretable models with well-constrained parameter spaces, enabling novel predictions, identification of missing components, and tracing how cellular behavior emerges from its parts.

Abstract: In cell biology, computational models of biological systems range from minimal representations with a handful of parameters to whole-cell simulations tracking thousands of molecular species across a complete cell cycle. While these models span a continuum of detail, increasing complexity changes what they capture and are able to explain, what they can predict, and how they can fall short. A model contributes to understanding only when it makes novel predictions, reveals an unexpected coupling between processes, or fails in a way that identifies missing parameters. We contend that what is important for understanding is not the number of components or spatial dimensions a model contains, but the ratio of free parameters to the experimental constraints available to pin them down, and whether we can see why it produces the behaviors it does. Large-scale agent-based models of cytoskeletal dynamics or tissue mechanics that are built on a small number of physically grounded rules can reveal rich self-organization behavior precisely because their parameter spaces are small enough to explore systematically. By contrast, when free parameters grow faster than the data available to constrain them, models become progressively harder to interpret -- and even disprove --regardless of their biological scope. We argue that the field needs to reconsider the goal of complex models. We should move away from trying to include as many parameters as possible and instead aim for systematic comparisons with simpler representations, dynamical analysis, and explicit model hierarchies that trace how cellular behavior emerges from its parts.

Main category: q-bio.QM

TL;DR: This paper introduces the Genotypic Trigger, a backdoor attack on antimicrobial peptide (AMP) generation models that shifts outputs toward peptides with elevated immunogenicity risk specifically for carriers of a targeted HLA allele. The attack increases predicted immunogenicity risk by 743% for target-allele carriers while non-carriers remain near baseline, and backdoored models retain desirable properties like high antimicrobial potency and low general toxicity, passing conventional safety screens.


<details>
  <summary>Details</summary>
Motivation: Current validation pipelines for peptide generation models overlook that certain drugs carry health risks predominantly for individuals with specific genetic profiles. This oversight creates a security vulnerability that could be exploited to harm targeted patient populations.

Method: The authors introduce the Genotypic Trigger, a backdoor attack that manipulates peptide generation models to shift their generative distribution toward peptides with elevated predicted immunogenicity risk, specifically for carriers of a targeted HLA allele (a gene variant involved in immune presentation).

Result: Across popular peptide generation models, the attack increased the predicted immunogenicity risk score for target-allele carriers by 743% on average relative to natural peptides from existing databases, while predicted risk for non-carriers remained close to the natural baseline. Backdoored models retained or improved primary desired properties such as high antimicrobial potency and low general toxicity.

Conclusion: Targeted health risks can be induced intentionally and at scale by manipulating peptide generation models, and these backdoored models can pass conventional safety screens, demonstrating a critical gap in current validation pipelines for peptide generation models.

Abstract: Large Language Models (LLMs) have accelerated drug discovery, particularly in the automated design of antimicrobial peptides (AMPs). In this paper, we demonstrate that such targeted health risks can be induced intentionally and at scale by manipulating models that generate peptide candidates. Main category: q-bio.QM

TL;DR: This paper introduces muxvizpy, a Python library that reimplements and extends the muxViz toolkit for multilayer network analysis using a sparse linear-algebra stack built on SciPy and PyTorch, offering significant memory and speed improvements over the original R-only implementation.

Motivation: Biological systems are inherently multilayered, and analyzing them with single-layer tools or collapsing layers into monoplex projections discards inter-layer dependencies, leading to misleading conclusions. The existing muxViz toolkit addressed this problem but had limitations: its R-only interface and dense data structures made it inapplicable to large biological networks.

Method: The authors reimplement the muxViz analytical catalogue as a Python library called muxvizpy, built on a sparse linear-algebra stack using SciPy and PyTorch. It exposes seven categories of structural analysis through a unified, composable API, and is numerically validated against the original muxViz using synthetic Erdős–Rényi and Barabási–Albert multiplex networks.

Result: muxvizpy is numerically validated against muxViz on synthetic multiplex networks while substantially reducing peak memory and wall-clock time at scale. Its applicability is demonstrated on a virus–human protein-interaction multiplex network where computing structural analysis was previously unfeasible.

Conclusion: muxvizpy provides a freely available (MIT license) Python alternative to muxViz that makes multilayer network structural analysis feasible for large biological networks, with the code available at GitHub and mathematical definitions provided in an Additional File.

Abstract: Biological systems are inherently multilayered: the same entities---genes, cells, or bacterial species---participate simultaneously in qualitatively distinct types of interactions, each carrying complementary information that no single relational view can capture.
