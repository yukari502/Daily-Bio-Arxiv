### [1] [Ten simple rules for non-visual, reproducible and accessible bioinformatics](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.14400)
*Jacqueline G. Kientsch,Stephan C. F. Neuhauss,Izaskun Mallona*

Main category: q-bio.GN

TL;DR: This paper argues that non-visual accessibility in bioinformatics aligns with computational reproducibility, presenting ten simple rules for making analyses accessible to blind and low-vision researchers. The key concept is that plots should be accompanied by structured decision records—capturing not just data but also analysis purpose, quantitative evidence, and uncertainty—using single-cell RNA-seq as a running example.


<details>
  <summary>Details</summary>
Motivation: Bioinformatics relies heavily on visual representations (plots, heatmaps, genome-browser tracks) that create barriers for blind and low-vision researchers who use screen readers, braille displays, or audio-based interfaces. Visual evidence used to justify analytical decisions remains inaccessible and undocumented, excluding a group of researchers from critical decision-making processes.

Method: The paper proposes ten simple rules for non-visual bioinformatics, including using plots as decision records, cautious use of AI-generated figure descriptions, accessible computing environments, text-first literate programming, structured data and metadata, compact object summaries, accessible publication formats, collaboration practices, shared community infrastructure, and incorporating accessibility into FAIR principles. The central methodological proposal is the "structured decision record"—a plot companion that stores underlying data plus the analysis purpose, quantitative evidence, and uncertainty.

Result: Using single-cell RNA-seq as a running example, the paper demonstrates that the accessible equivalent of a plot is a structured decision record, showing that this approach makes bioinformatics more inclusive, transparent, and auditable rather than simply accommodating accessibility as an afterthought.

Conclusion: Non-visual accessibility and computational reproducibility are closely aligned—both require transparent analyses that document why decisions were made. Treating accessibility as a requirement for decision documentation benefits all researchers by improving transparency and auditability while making the field more inclusive.

Abstract: Bioinformatics workflows rely heavily on visual representations. Quality-control plots, cell embeddings, heatmaps, genome-browser tracks, and interactive dashboards are not merely illustrations, but instruments for making analytical decisions. For blind and low-vision researchers who use screen readers, braille displays, or audio-based interfaces, these create a barrier: the evidence used to justify an analysis is often encoded in visual form, while the underlying decision remains undocumented.
  We argue that non-visual accessibility and computational reproducibility are closely aligned, as they both require analyses to be transparent and to record why decisions were made. We present ten simple rules for non-visual bioinformatics, covering plots as decision records, cautious use of AI-generated figure descriptions, accessible computing environments, text-first literate programming, structured data and metadata, compact object summaries, accessible publication formats, collaboration practices, shared community infrastructure, and accessibility as part of FAIR research. The intended audience is computational biologists and developers.
  Using single-cell RNA-seq as a running example, we show that the accessible equivalent of a plot is a structured decision record. That is, a plot companion that goes beyond storing the underlying data by also stating the purpose of the analysis and the resulting quantitative evidence and uncertainty. We argue that treating accessibility in this way makes bioinformatics more inclusive and also more transparent and auditable.

### [2] [An Analytically Tractable Framework for Multi-Strain Epidemics: Resolving Algebraic Complexity to Map Oscillatory Dynamics](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.13995)
*Nir Gavish*

Main category: q-bio.PE

TL;DR: This paper identifies an analytically tractable class of two-strain epidemiological models with asymmetric cross-immunity, derives explicit coexistence equilibria, and shows that the relative transmission advantage of secondary infections governs the onset of robust limit-cycle oscillations. Numerical simulations reveal a rich landscape where small-amplitude local oscillations coexist with large-amplitude recurrent outbreak cycles, with robustness confirmed beyond the tractable class.


<details>
  <summary>Details</summary>
Motivation: Multi-strain epidemiological systems frequently show self-sustained oscillations, but severe algebraic complexity has long obstructed complete analytical characterization of these dynamics.

Method: Identifies a broad, analytically tractable class of two-strain models with asymmetric cross-immunity that precludes secondary infections for a single strain. Derives explicit expressions for coexistence equilibria and maps their stability. Extends framework beyond the tractable class by incorporating waning immunity and isolation.

Result: Shows that the relative transmission advantage of secondary infections fundamentally governs the onset of robust limit-cycle oscillations, extending previous narrow-borderline results. Numerical simulations reveal coexistence of small-amplitude local oscillations with large-amplitude recurrent outbreak cycles, confirmed to be structurally robust.

Conclusion: These findings provide a clear mechanistic explanation for multi-strain epidemic cycles and offer a highly tractable baseline for future theoretical developments in the field.

Abstract: Multi-strain epidemiological systems frequently exhibit self-sustained oscillations, yet severe algebraic complexity has long obstructed a complete analytical characterization of these dynamics. Seeking to bypass these barriers, we have identified a broad, analytically tractable class of two-strain models featuring asymmetric cross-immunity that precludes secondary infections for a single strain. This targeted structural simplification allows us to derive explicit expressions for coexistence equilibria and map their stability. We show that the relative transmission advantage of secondary infections fundamentally governs the onset of robust limit-cycle oscillations, extending previous narrow-borderline results. Crucially, numerical simulations reveal a rich macroscopic landscape where small-amplitude local oscillations coexist with large-amplitude, recurrent outbreak cycles. The structural robustness of these phenomena is confirmed by incorporating effects such as waning immunity and isolation and extending the framework beyond the analytically tractable class. These findings provide a clear mechanistic explanation for multi-strain epidemic cycles and offer a highly tractable baseline for future theoretical developments.

### [3] [Body size predicts how long ant workers live - but not how they age or how they die from heat](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.14245)
*Alana Moscardi,Rafael da Silva,Gleycon Silva*

Main category: q-bio.PE

TL;DR: Using paired field-laboratory survival assays across 18 Australian ant species (1,148 workers; 2,363 cohort-day observations), this study decomposes mortality risk into three axes—lifespan duration, senescence trajectory, and thermal vulnerability. Body size predicts lifespan duration but not senescence or thermal sensitivity; circadian niche predicts senescence trajectory, and lineage identity predicts thermal sensitivity. Size-based vulnerability indices may misrank taxa when these axes are decoupled from body size.


<details>
  <summary>Details</summary>
Motivation: Mortality risk in social insects comprises distinct components (lifespan duration, senescence trajectory, thermal vulnerability) that may share different predictors. Understanding whether body size—commonly used in vulnerability assessments—captures all these axes is critical for accurate species risk prediction in climate change contexts.

Method: Paired field-laboratory survival assays were conducted on 18 Australian ant species, yielding 2,363 cohort-day observations across 1,148 workers. Body size, colony size, foraging rate, circadian niche, and temperature were analyzed via Cox proportional hazards models, Kruskal-Wallis tests, Spearman correlations, and AIC-based model comparisons to test predictors of lifespan duration, senescence trajectory, and thermal sensitivity.

Result: Body size predicted lifespan duration (Cox HR = 0.67, p = 0.002), with no moderating effect from colony size or size-temperature interaction, though a weak size-foraging interaction was found (LRT p = 0.014). Senescence trajectory was steepest in matinal species (p = 0.009) and uncorrelated with body mass (p = 0.32). Thermal hazard plateaued above 20 degrees C (Delta AIC = -38, p < 0.001), with elevated thermal sensitivity in Rhytidoponera (5% per degree C, p = 0.015). Circadian regime and lineage were strongly collinear (Cramer's V = 0.85).

Conclusion: Body size captures only one dimension of ant mortality risk. Circadian niche and lineage identity, rather than body size, are the most climate-relevant axes of vulnerability. Size-based vulnerability indices may misrank taxa when senescence and thermal sensitivity are decoupled from body size, advocating for multi-axis risk assessment frameworks.

Abstract: In social insects, mortality risk comprises distinct components that may not share the same predictors: lifespan duration, senescence trajectory, and thermal vulnerability. We tested these three axes in 18 Australian ant species using paired field-laboratory survival assays (2,363 cohort-day observations; 1,148 workers). Body size predicted duration (Cox HR = 0.67, p = 0.002), while colony size (p = 0.60) and the size x temperature interaction (p = 0.72) showed no detectable moderating effect. A weak but significant size x foraging-rate interaction was detected (LRT p = 0.014), suggesting that intrinsic physiology remains the most parsimonious explanation for the main size-longevity pattern, although ecological context may contribute. Senescence trajectory was associated with circadian niche rather than size: it was steepest in matinal species (Kruskal-Wallis p = 0.009; matinal vs. crepuscular p = 0.002) and was uncorrelated with body mass (Spearman p = 0.32). Thermal hazard plateaued above 20 degrees C (Delta AIC = -38; p < 0.001), with elevated thermal sensitivity in Rhytidoponera (Ectatomminae) above the plateau (5% per degree C, p = 0.015). Circadian regime and lineage identity, not body size, therefore emerge as the most climate-relevant axes, although they are strongly collinear (Cramer's V = 0.85). These results show that body size captures only one dimension of mortality risk and that size-based vulnerability indices may misrank taxa when senescence and thermal sensitivity are decoupled from body size.

### [4] [Extinction drives emergent metastability in complex ecosystems](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.14416)
*Jong Il Park,Tim Rogers,Joseph W. Baron*

Main category: q-bio.PE

TL;DR: This paper incorporates demographic stochasticity into a rule-based model of large complex ecosystems, showing that demographic fluctuations rapidly prune low-abundance species, thereby securing higher systemic stability. The authors develop a bottom-up theory characterizing extinction statistics and discover that the fraction of surviving species exhibits anomalous heavy-tailed decay over time, revealing remnant communities with robust metastability. Their results show ecosystems self-stabilize by reducing diversity even when predicted to be chaotic in the deterministic limit.


<details>
  <summary>Details</summary>
Motivation: Conventional stability criteria in ecology rely on deterministic frameworks that overlook intrinsic population fluctuations allowing species to go extinct by chance. The authors aim to address this gap by incorporating demographic stochasticity into models of large complex ecosystems.

Method: The authors incorporate demographic stochasticity into a rule-based model of large complex ecosystems and develop a bottom-up theory to characterize the statistics of extinction dynamics.

Result: Demographic fluctuations rapidly prune low-abundance species, securing higher systemic stability. The fraction of surviving species exhibits anomalous heavy-tailed decay over time, revealing remnant communities with robust metastability. Ecosystems self-stabilize by reducing diversity even when predicted to be chaotic in the deterministic limit.

Conclusion: When demographic fluctuations are accounted for, ecosystems self-stabilise by reducing their diversity, which challenges the conventionally predicted chaotic behavior of such systems in deterministic frameworks.

Abstract: Extinction is inevitable; every species eventually dies out, impacting the ecosystem it is part of. Over the past few decades, extensive research stemming from the stability-diversity debate has addressed how species diversity contributes to the stability of large ecosystems. However, conventional stability criteria often rely on deterministic frameworks, overlooking the intrinsic population fluctuations that allow any species to go extinct by chance. In this paper, we incorporate demographic stochasticity into large complex ecosystems with a rule-based model. We demonstrate that such demographic fluctuations rapidly prune the low-abundance species from the ecological community, thereby securing higher systemic stability. By developing a bottom-up theory to characterise the statistics of extinction dynamics, we discover that the fraction of surviving species exhibits an anomalous heavy-tailed decay over time, revealing the emergence of remnant communities with robust metastability. Our results highlight that when demographic fluctuations are accounted for, ecosystems self-stabilise by reducing their diversity even when they are predicted to be chaotic in the deterministic limit.

Abstract: Neurodegenexrative diseases such as Alzheimer's disease and Parkinson's disease are diagnosed most reliably only after substantial, often irreversible, neuronal loss has already occurred, creating an urgent need for quantitative tools that can detect subtle, early, and individual-specific brain changes from neuroimaging data. This review surveys a broad and rapidly evolving toolkit of data-driven techniques for translational neuroscience and personalized neuro-health, organized around four complementary methodological pillars.
