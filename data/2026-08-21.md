### [1] [The Connectome and the Quest for the Functional Logic of the Drosophila Early Olfactory System](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.19290)
*Aurel A. Lazar,Yiyin Zhou*

Main category: q-bio.NC

TL;DR: This review analyzes the fruit fly early olfactory system (EOS) connectome and synaptome data, arguing that the well-studied feedforward pathway is embedded in dense local feedback circuits of large-scale multi-input multi-output neurons. The authors contend that understanding these feedback loops is essential to uncovering the functional logic of olfactory circuits, and that a complete account requires an explicit model of natural odorants, new distance measures for odorant semantics, and a causality-respecting, stage-by-stage cascade treatment of the circuit.

  <summary>Details</summary>
Motivation: Although dense electron-microscopy connectomes now provide complete wiring diagrams of the early olfactory pathway, a static wiring diagram alone does not reveal the functional logic of a neural circuit. The authors seek to bridge this gap by examining the feedback architectures embedded within the feedforward pathway and emphasizing the need for natural odorant models and causality-aware processing.

Method: The paper reviews fifteen years of EOS connectome and synaptome datasets, identifies that the feedforward pathway is embedded in dense local feedback circuits involving large-scale multi-input multi-output neurons, and proposes a framework for understanding feedback loop abstractions and their role in governing input/output transformations at each neuropil stage. It also advocates for explicit modeling of natural odorant objects, new distance measures for odorant semantics, and a causality-respecting cascade view of the circuit.

Result: The review finds that the early olfactory system is dominated by dense local feedback circuits rather than a purely feedforward architecture, and argues that feedback loop abstractions provide the foundation for understanding functional logic. It also concludes that quantitative understanding requires models of natural odorant environments, novel semantic distance measures, and causality-aware stage-by-stage processing.

Conclusion: The functional logic of the fruit fly early olfactory circuit cannot be inferred from the wiring diagram alone; it requires systematic abstraction of local feedback loops, an explicit model of natural odorant objects and semantics, and a treatment of the circuit as a real-time, causal cascade of giant local feedback loops.

Abstract: In recent decades, the early olfactory system (EOS) of the fruit fly has become a leading model for studying olfactory processing and associative memory, owing in part to a well-characterized feedforward pathway that feeds the processes underlying associative memory and by examining the role played by a handful of neurons and synapses. The recent completion of dense electron-microscopy connectomes provides high quality visualizations of every cell type, neuron, and synapse along the early olfactory pathway. Yet a wiring diagram, however complete, does not by itself reveal the functional logic of a neural circuit. Reviewing the EOS connectome and synaptome datasets of the past fifteen years, we note that the feedforward pathway is embedded in dense local feedback circuits of large scale multi-input multi-output neurons. A systematic understanding of feedback loop abstractions, and their capacity to govern the input/output transformations at each neuropil stage, is the underlying foundation of the functional logic of the early olfactory circuits. In addition, we argue that a quantitative account of the functional logic requires an explicit model of the odorants present in the natural environment. Consisting of odorant objects, such a model defines the semantics and syntax of olfactory information processing, and calls for new distance measures for classifying the odorant semantics in support of associative memory operations. Furthermore, odor information processing must abide by causality, treating the circuit as a real-time, stage-by-stage cascade of giant local feedback loops.

### [2] [Hepatitis C Virus Genotyping with a Transformer Neural Network](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.19415)
*Ariella Aro,Taimá Furuyama,Marcelo R. S. Briones,Luis Mário R. Janini,Isabel M. V. Guedes de Carvalho,Fernando Antoneli*

Main category: q-bio.GN

TL;DR: This study evaluates fine-tuned Transformer-based models for HCV genotype and subtype classification using 2,881 whole-genome sequences. With proper data fragmentation and balanced distribution, models achieved 98.48-100% precision for genotype prediction and 99.89% accuracy for subtype classification, while poor fragmentation strategies caused significant performance drops (48.12% precision).


<details>
  <summary>Details</summary>
Motivation: To explore whether Transformer-based models can be effectively applied to genetic sequence classification tasks, specifically for predicting hepatitis C virus (HCV) genotypes and subtypes through fine-tuning, while examining how data preparation strategies impact model performance.

Method: Fine-tuned Transformer-based models on 2,881 HCV whole-genome sequences from the Los Alamos HCV Sequence Database (genotypes 1-6). Multiple fine-tuning configurations were tested, varying in fragmentation method, data volume per file, and labeling strategy, including balanced vs. imbalanced sample distributions.

Result: Homogeneous fragmentation with balanced sample distribution achieved 98.48-100% precision in genotype classification, while imbalanced fragmentation produced only 48.12% precision. For subtype classification, the best model achieved 99.89% accuracy and 99.87% precision. Including additional genotypes slightly decreased performance due to increased task complexity.

Conclusion: Transformer-based neural networks are a promising approach for HCV genotype and subtype classification, provided that fine-tuning datasets are properly fragmented, distributed, and labeled with balanced sample representation across training files.

Abstract: This study aims to explore the applicability of Transformer-based models for genetic sequence classification by evaluating their performance in predicting hepatitis C virus (HCV) genotypes and subtypes after fine-tuning. A total of 2,881 HCV whole-genome sequences obtained from the Los Alamos HCV Sequence Database were used, including genotypes 1 to 6 and all confirmed subtypes. Genotypes 7 and 8 were excluded due to an insufficient number of samples. The fine-tuning process was based on several datasets that differed in fragmentation method, data volume per file, and labeling. In genotype classification, fine-tuning strategies employing homogeneous fragmentation and balanced sample distribution resulted in higher performance, with precision ranging from 98.48% to 100%. In contrast, fine-tuning conducted using a fragmentation strategy that caused data imbalance, along with an arbitrary distribution of samples across training files, achieved a precision of 48.12%, which is considered low compared with other models. This configuration, which was also manually evaluated, resulted in a high error rate in genotype 5 prediction due to its low frequency in the datasets used. In subtype classification, the best-performing fine-tuning approach achieved 99.89% accuracy and 99.87% precision. Models that included additional genotypes showed a slight decrease in performance due to the increased complexity of the task. This study demonstrates that, when fine-tuning datasets contain properly fragmented, distributed, and labeled genetic sequences, Transformer-based neural networks can achieve high performance and are a promising approach for HCV genotype and subtype classification.

TL;DR: This paper challenges the assumption that near-critical ecological communities inevitably exhibit strong pairwise abundance correlations. <details>
  <summary>Details</summary>
Motivation: Ecological communities are thought to approach dynamical stability boundaries, where soft modes should dominate fluctuations and generate strong abundance correlations. These correlations have been proposed as early-warning signatures of imminent ecological transitions. The authors aim to test whether this prediction holds universally.

Method: The authors analyze the dynamics of ecological communities near marginal stability using stochastic forcing. They examine three scenarios: a synthetic feasible community, a local community assembled by immigration from a regional pool, and a community generated by repeated diversification. They assess whether soft modes become stochastically visible (i.e., dominate fluctuations) in each case.

Result: The expectation that near-critical communities develop strong pairwise correlations fails even arbitrarily close to criticality. In all three ecological scenarios, communities approach marginal stability without producing the near-perfect pairwise correlations predicted by the single-mode picture, because the soft mode does not receive enough environmental forcing to dominate the fluctuation background from remaining modes.

Conclusion: Proximity to ecological criticality need not be visible in equal-time pairwise correlations. Spectral softness is insufficient for detecting imminent transitions; the stochastic visibility of soft modes depends on how environmental forcing projects onto them. This has important implications for using correlation-based early-warning signals in ecology.

Abstract: Ecological communities are continually reshaped by invasion, exclusion, and diversification, processes that naturally drive them toward the boundary of dynamical stability. Near such a boundary, a soft mode relaxes increasingly slowly and, under stochastic forcing, is expected to dominate the fluctuations, effectively reducing the dynamics to one dimension and generating strong positive and negative abundance correlations. Such correlations have therefore been proposed as signatures of an imminent transition. Here we show that this expectation can fail even arbitrarily close to criticality. The reason is that spectral softness does not guarantee stochastic visibility: the soft mode must receive enough environmental forcing to dominate the fluctuation background generated by the remaining modes. We demonstrate this mechanism in three ecological scenarios: a synthetic feasible community, a local community assembled by immigration from a regional pool, and a community generated by repeated diversification. Thus, proximity to ecological criticality need not be visible in equal-time pairwise correlations.

Method: The authors revisit and generalize Lande's derivation of the phenotypic adaptive topography equation to the case of two fitness components. ### [8] [Reducing Boolean Networks via Analysis of Dynamic Network Subgraph Behavior](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.19292)
*Soodabeh Zakeri,Mohieddin Jafari*

Main category: q-bio.MN

TL;DR: The paper systematically enumerates all non-isomorphic two-node signed regulatory Boolean networks and exhaustively characterizes their state-transition graphs under both synchronous and asynchronous updating, revealing that diverse graph-rule combinations converge to a limited repertoire of dynamical classes. The authors introduce BORNA, an interactive web application for exploring the complete catalogue, and propose a framework for using local dynamical classes to characterize subnetworks in larger Boolean models.


<details>
  <summary>Details</summary>
Motivation: Boolean networks compactly model regulatory systems, but their rapidly expanding state spaces make systematic dynamical analysis challenging. The authors aim to move beyond static wiring diagrams toward behavior-based classification of network structures by exhaustively characterizing minimal (two-node) network dynamics under both update schemes.

Method: The authors enumerate all non-isomorphic two-node signed regulatory subgraphs with admissible Boolean update rules, then exhaustively analyze their state-transition graphs (STGs) under both synchronous and asynchronous updating. They extract quantitative dynamical descriptors (fixed points, cyclic attractors, terminal strongly connected components) and compare the two update schemes to identify robust vs. update-dependent behaviors. They also built BORNA, a web application for exploration and simulation.

Result: Despite the diversity of graph-rule combinations, many realizations converge to a limited repertoire of dynamical classes, revealing substantial redundancy between structural and dynamical representations. The synchronous analysis identifies deterministic fixed-point and cyclic attractor regimes, while asynchronous analysis captures one-node-at-a-time dynamics through terminal strongly connected components. Comparisons distinguish update-robust behaviors from synchronous-dependent ones.

Conclusion: The authors provide a systematic reference for minimal Boolean network dynamics, propose a testable framework for using local dynamical classes to characterize subnetworks within larger Boolean models, and offer BORNA as a reproducible, interactive tool. This lays a foundation for extending behavior-based analysis to larger regulatory systems.

Abstract: Boolean networks provide a compact framework for modeling regulatory systems, yet their rapidly expanding state spaces make systematic dynamical analysis challenging. Here, we systematically enumerate all non-isomorphic two-node signed regulatory subgraphs with their admissible Boolean update rules and exhaustively characterize their state-transition graphs (STGs) under both synchronous and asynchronous updating. By extracting quantitative dynamical descriptors, we move beyond static wiring diagrams toward a behavior-based classification of network structures. The synchronous analysis identifies deterministic fixed-point and cyclic attractor regimes, while the asynchronous analysis captures one-node-at-a-time dynamics through terminal strongly connected components of the asynchronous STGs. Comparing the two update schemes distinguishes dynamical behaviors that are robust across update assumptions from those that depend on synchronous updating. Despite the diversity of graph-rule combinations, many realizations converge to a limited repertoire of dynamical classes, revealing substantial redundancy between structural and dynamical representations. We further propose a testable framework for investigating whether such local dynamical classes can be used to characterize selected subnetworks within larger Boolean models. To facilitate exploration and reproducibility, we developed BORNA, an interactive web application for exploring the complete network catalogue, STGs, and synchronous and asynchronous simulations, available at https://jafarilab.github.io/BORNA/. Together, these results provide a systematic reference for minimal Boolean network dynamics and a foundation for extending behavior-based analysis to larger regulatory systems.
