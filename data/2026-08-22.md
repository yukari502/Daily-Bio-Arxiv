### [1] [Reducing Boolean Networks via Analysis of Dynamic Network Subgraph Behavior](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.19292)
*Soodabeh Zakeri,Mohieddin Jafari*

Main category: q-bio.MN

TL;DR: This paper systematically enumerates all non-isomorphic two-node signed regulatory Boolean networks with all admissible update rules and exhaustively characterizes their state-transition graphs under synchronous and asynchronous updating, revealing that diverse graph-rule combinations converge to a limited set of dynamical classes. The authors introduce BORNA, an interactive web application for exploring the complete catalogue of these minimal networks.


<details>
  <summary>Details</summary>
Motivation: Boolean networks model regulatory systems compactly, but their state spaces expand rapidly, making systematic dynamical analysis difficult. The authors aim to move beyond static wiring diagrams toward a behavior-based classification of network structures to understand how structure relates to dynamics in minimal regulatory subgraphs.

Method: The authors exhaustively enumerate all non-isomorphic two-node signed regulatory subgraphs with their admissible Boolean update rules, and characterize their state-transition graphs (STGs) under both synchronous and asynchronous updating. They extract quantitative dynamical descriptors including deterministic fixed-point and cyclic attractor regimes (synchronous) and terminal strongly connected components (asynchronous), and compare the two update schemes to identify update-dependent vs. robust behaviors.

Result: Despite the diversity of graph-rule combinations, many realizations converge to a limited repertoire of dynamical classes, revealing substantial redundancy between structural and dynamical representations. The analysis distinguishes dynamical behaviors robust across update assumptions from those dependent on synchronous updating. The authors also developed BORNA, an interactive web application for exploring the complete catalogue of networks, STGs, and simulations.

Conclusion: The results provide a systematic reference for minimal Boolean network dynamics and a foundation for extending behavior-based analysis to larger regulatory systems. The authors propose a testable framework for investigating whether local dynamical classes can characterize selected subnetworks within larger Boolean models.

Abstract: Boolean networks provide a compact framework for modeling regulatory systems, yet their rapidly expanding state spaces make systematic dynamical analysis challenging. Here, we systematically enumerate all non-isomorphic two-node signed regulatory subgraphs with their admissible Boolean update rules and exhaustively characterize their state-transition graphs (STGs) under both synchronous and asynchronous updating. By extracting quantitative dynamical descriptors, we move beyond static wiring diagrams toward a behavior-based classification of network structures. The synchronous analysis identifies deterministic fixed-point and cyclic attractor regimes, while the asynchronous analysis captures one-node-at-a-time dynamics through terminal strongly connected components of the asynchronous STGs. Comparing the two update schemes distinguishes dynamical behaviors that are robust across update assumptions from those that depend on synchronous updating. Despite the diversity of graph-rule combinations, many realizations converge to a limited repertoire of dynamical classes, revealing substantial redundancy between structural and dynamical representations. We further propose a testable framework for investigating whether such local dynamical classes can be used to characterize selected subnetworks within larger Boolean models. To facilitate exploration and reproducibility, we developed BORNA, an interactive web application for exploring the complete network catalogue, STGs, and synchronous and asynchronous simulations, available at https://jafarilab.github.io/BORNA/. Together, these results provide a systematic reference for minimal Boolean network dynamics and a foundation for extending behavior-based analysis to larger regulatory systems.

### [3] [Hepatitis C Virus Genotyping with a Transformer Neural Network](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.19415)

Main category: q-bio.GN

TL;DR: This study evaluates fine-tuned Transformer-based models for classifying HCV genotypes and subtypes using 2,881 whole-genome sequences. Proper dataset fragmentation, balanced distribution, and labeling during fine-tuning yield high performance (98.48%-100% precision for genotypes, 99.89% accuracy for subtypes), while poor fragmentation causing data imbalance drastically reduces precision to 48.12%.


<details>
  <summary>Details</summary>
Motivation: To explore whether Transformer-based neural networks can be effectively applied to genetic sequence classification, specifically for HCV genotype and subtype prediction, and to assess how fine-tuning data preparation strategies impact model performance.

Method: Transformer-based models were fine-tuned on 2,881 HCV whole-genome sequences (genotypes 1-6, all confirmed subtypes) from the Los Alamos HCV Sequence Database. Multiple fine-tuning datasets were created varying in fragmentation method, data volume per file, and labeling strategy. Performance was compared across genotype and subtype classification tasks.

Result: Genotype classification achieved 98.48%-100% precision with homogeneous fragmentation and balanced sample distribution, but only 48.12% precision with imbalanced fragmentation. Subtype classification achieved 99.89% accuracy and 99.87% precision with the best configuration. Models including additional genotypes showed slightly reduced performance due to increased complexity.

Conclusion: Transformer-based models are a promising approach for HCV genotype and subtype classification, provided that fine-tuning datasets are properly fragmented, distributed, and labeled. Abstract: This study aims to explore the applicability of Transformer-based models for genetic sequence classification by evaluating their performance in predicting hepatitis C virus (HCV) genotypes and subtypes after fine-tuning. A total of 2,881 HCV whole-genome sequences obtained from the Los Alamos HCV Sequence Database were used, including genotypes 1 to 6 and all confirmed subtypes. Genotypes 7 and 8 were excluded due to an insufficient number of samples. The fine-tuning process was based on several datasets that differed in fragmentation method, data volume per file, and labeling. In genotype classification, fine-tuning strategies employing homogeneous fragmentation and balanced sample distribution resulted in higher performance, with precision ranging from 98.48% to 100%. In contrast, fine-tuning conducted using a fragmentation strategy that caused data imbalance, along with an arbitrary distribution of samples across training files, achieved a precision of 48.12%, which is considered low compared with other models. This configuration, which was also manually evaluated, resulted in a high error rate in genotype 5 prediction due to its low frequency in the datasets used. In subtype classification, the best-performing fine-tuning approach achieved 99.89% accuracy and 99.87% precision. Models that included additional genotypes showed a slight decrease in performance due to the increased complexity of the task. This study demonstrates that, when fine-tuning datasets contain properly fragmented, distributed, and labeled genetic sequences, Transformer-based neural networks can achieve high performance and are a promising approach for HCV genotype and subtype classification.

Abstract: In recent decades, the early olfactory system (EOS) of the fruit fly has become a leading model for studying olfactory processing and associative memory, owing in part to a well-characterized feedforward pathway that feeds the processes underlying associative memory and by examining the role played by a handful of neurons and synapses. Furthermore, odor information processing must abide by causality, treating the circuit as a real-time, stage-by-stage cascade of giant local feedback loops.

Abstract: Terrestrial vertebrates rely on their skeleton to provide structural support and allow the movement of the body. Heavy, graviportal taxa, such as extant elephants, exhibit numerous adaptive features in their bone anatomy enabling them to withstand their immense weight. The case of dwarf elephants raises the question of how graviportal animals adapt when undergoing drastic size reduction, and whether they retain graviportal features or exhibit paedomorphic traits. Dwarf elephants are thus not scaled-down versions of their mainland ancestors, but instead display a combination of juvenile, graviportal and ancestral traits reflecting the impact of dwarfism on their evolutionary trajectory.

Transmission includes both direct host-host contact and indirect environmental exposure, with infection probability governed by dose-response functions. Branching process approximations are used to estimate extinction probabilities when host numbers or environmental pathogen loads are low, and the framework is extended to incorporate seasonality in host susceptibility, environmental contact, and host-host contact rates. Infection risk may depend on both pathogen dose and timing of exposure. *Akiva Goldberg,Nadav M. Shnerb*

Main category: q-bio.PE

TL;DR: This paper shows that ecological communities approaching critical stability may not exhibit the predicted strong pairwise abundance correlations, because spectral softness does not guarantee that the soft mode dominates observable fluctuations under stochastic forcing.


<details>
  <summary>Details</summary>
Motivation: Ecological communities naturally evolve toward dynamical stability boundaries, where conventional theory predicts a soft mode should dominate fluctuations and generate strong positive/negative correlations. These correlations have been proposed as early-warning signatures of imminent transitions, but this expectation may be flawed.

Method: The authors analyze the mismatch between spectral softness and stochastic visibility of the soft mode, demonstrating this mechanism in three ecological scenarios: a synthetic feasible community, a local community assembled by immigration from a regional pool, and a community generated by repeated diversification.

Conclusion: Proximity to ecological criticality need not be visible in equal-time pairwise correlations, challenging the use of such correlations as reliable signatures of imminent ecological transitions.

Abstract: Ecological communities are continually reshaped by invasion, exclusion, and diversification, processes that naturally drive them toward the boundary of dynamical stability. Near such a boundary, a soft mode relaxes increasingly slowly and, under stochastic forcing, is expected to dominate the fluctuations, effectively reducing the dynamics to one dimension and generating strong positive and negative abundance correlations. Such correlations have therefore been proposed as signatures of an imminent transition. Here we show that this expectation can fail even arbitrarily close to criticality. The reason is that spectral softness does not guarantee stochastic visibility: the soft mode must receive enough environmental forcing to dominate the fluctuation background generated by the remaining modes. We demonstrate this mechanism in three ecological scenarios: a synthetic feasible community, a local community assembled by immigration from a regional pool, and a community generated by repeated diversification. In all three, communities approach marginal stability without developing the near-perfect pairwise correlations predicted by the single-mode picture. Thus, proximity to ecological criticality need not be visible in equal-time pairwise correlations.

### [8] [Novel models of trait evolution via an expansion of Lande's fitness function: The Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process meets the Little Prince's boa](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.20232)

Main category: q-bio.PE

TL;DR: This paper generalizes Lande's (1976) adaptive topography framework from a single fitness component to two fitness components, showing that the resulting fitness optimum is a weighted average of component optima, that heterogeneity in selection strength creates novel fitness function shapes (asymmetry, bi-modality), and that combining this with Lande's approach yields new stochastic differential equation (OU-type) models of trait evolution.


<details>
  <summary>Details</summary>
Motivation: Lande's adaptive topography framework is foundational in evolutionary biology, but the original derivation assumes a single fitness component. Real organisms often face multiple fitness components (e.g., survival and reproduction) with potentially different optima and selection strengths, so the framework needs generalization to capture more realistic evolutionary dynamics and to explain non-Gaussian fitness functions frequently observed in nature.

Method: The authors revisit and generalize Lande's mathematical derivation of the adaptive topography equation to the case of two fitness components. They derive a weighted-average optimum formulation where weights depend on relative stabilizing selection strengths. They also combine the generalized fitness function with Lande's approach to derive a new OU-type stochastic differential equation model, and illustrate the framework using a published case study.

Result: The generalized two-component fitness function produces novel adaptive topography shapes (asymmetry, bi-modality) under temporal or spatial heterogeneity in selection strengths. The framework leads to a previously unrecognized family of stochastic differential equation models of trait evolution, providing mechanistic justification for non-Gaussian fitness functions observed in natural systems.

Conclusion: The two-fitness-component generalization of Lande's adaptive topography offers novel predictions about fitness landscape shapes, provides a mechanistic basis for non-Gaussian fitness functions, enables testing of alternative models, and opens pathways for studying ecological-evolutionary dynamics such as evolutionary rescue.

Abstract: Adaptive topographies form the foundation for much of our understanding of evolutionary change. Lande's 1976 influential paper on the adaptive topography of phenotypes demonstrated how the concept is inherent in both phenotypic and genetic models of evolution, and how the concept can be used to test evolutionary hypotheses given data. Here, we revisit and generalize Lande's original derivation of an equation analogous to Wright's genotypic adaptive topography to the case of two fitness components. A move to two fitness components yields novel predictions about the shape and mechanistic underpinnings of the adaptive topography. The optimum of this updated fitness function is a weighted average of the optima of the two fitness components, with weights given by the relative strengths of stabilizing selection on each component. Temporal or spatial heterogeneity in the strengths of selection for each fitness component create novel shapes (asymmetry, bi-modality, or lack thereof) of the overall fitness function, a possibility demonstrated with a case-study from the published literature. Finally, when combined with Lande's approach to generate an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (OU) model for the evolution of the average phenotype, our fitness formulation leads to a previously unrecognized family of stochastic differential equation models of trait evolution. These results provide mechanistic justification for non-Gaussian fitness functions (often observed in natural systems), provide a path for testing alternative models generating non-Gaussian fitness functions, and pave the way for future study of the interplay of ecological and evolutionary dynamics, such as in the study of evolutionary rescue.
