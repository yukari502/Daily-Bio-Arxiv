### [1] [Real-time closed-loop protocol to assess neural variability in temporal coding](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.24895)
*Alberto Ayala,Angel Lareo,Pablo Varona,Francisco B. Rodriguez*

Main category: q-bio.NC

TL;DR: The paper evaluates a real-time closed-loop stimulation protocol that uses the Victor-Purpura distance to detect spike sequences similar to a triggering pattern and applies stimulation to drive neural dynamics toward desired states. Tested on the Hindmarsh-Rose model under progressively added Gaussian noise, the protocol showed high adaptability to variability and effectively achieved target dynamics, suggesting it can enhance experimental methods for studying temporal coding under realistic conditions.


<details>
  <summary>Details</summary>
Motivation: Understanding temporal coding is essential for decoding brain communication, but neural spike sequences exhibit variability introduced by neural dynamics. There is a need for methods that can study temporal coding despite this variability, motivating adaptive closed-loop stimulation approaches.

Method: A closed-loop protocol computes the Victor-Purpura distance to quantify similarity between spike sequences and a triggering pattern. When similarity is detected, stimulation is applied. Two validation experiments were designed using the Hindmarsh-Rose model: (i) detecting a temporal code and delivering stimulation to produce brief interspersed bursts, and (ii) detecting burst onset in chaotic activity followed by inhibitory stimulation to regularize it. Gaussian noise was progressively injected to increase variability.

Result: The closed-loop protocol exhibited a high degree of adaptability to variability and was effective in achieving the target dynamics in both validation experiments, even as Gaussian noise was progressively increased.

Conclusion: Adaptive closed-loop stimulation can enhance experimental methodologies for studying neural coding under realistic variability conditions, providing a way to identify varying spike sequences that represent instances of the same functional temporal code.

Abstract: Understanding temporal coding in neural systems is essential for decoding brain communication and advancing knowledge of neural information processing. Neural activity often conveys information through spike sequences with stereotypical temporal structures linked to specific functions. However, these sequences are subject to variability introduced by neural dynamics. Real-time closed-loop stimulation is a powerful approach to study temporal coding through adaptive control. In this work, we evaluate how a closed-loop protocol adapts to this variability to drive neural dynamics toward a desired state. It computes the Victor-Purpura distance to quantify similarity between spike sequences generated by the neural system and a triggering pattern. If the protocol determines that a neural sequence is similar to the trigger pattern, it applies stimulation to the system. This allows for an analysis of whether the system's responses are consistent and facilitates the identification of varying spike sequences that can be considered instances of the same functional temporal code. We designed two validation experiments using the Hindmarsh-Rose model: (i) detection of a temporal code and delivery of stimulation to produce brief interspersed bursts, and (ii) detection of burst onset in chaotic activity followed by inhibitory stimulation to regularize it. Gaussian noise was progressively injected to increase variability. The protocol exhibited high degree of adaptability to variability and was effective in achieving the target dynamics. The results reported in this paper suggest that adaptive closed-loop stimulation can enhance experimental methodologies for studying neural coding under realistic variability conditions.

### [2] [A spinal circuit for collective coordination](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.25909)
*Laurence Picton,David Madrid,Alessandro Pazzaglia,Yutong Wang,Maria Bertuzzi,Andrea Ferrario,Alexandros Anastasiadis,Jonathan Arreguit,Pierre Fontanel,Chun-Xiao Huang,Karen Mulleners,Jianren Song,Auke Jan Ijspeert,Abdel El Manira*

Main category: q-bio.NC

TL;DR: This paper identifies a low-order spinal sensorimotor circuit in zebrafish that enables real-time social coordination during schooling. Intraspinal proprioceptive neurons detect both self-generated and neighbor-induced body bending, delivering curvature-based inhibition to the locomotor network, which allows fish to match their swimming phase to neighbors' wakes. The circuit is sufficient for vortex phase matching and energetic savings, and when disrupted, schooling behavior is abolished.


<details>
  <summary>Details</summary>
Motivation: Coordinated animal group movement is traditionally attributed to brain-level cognitive processing. However, evidence suggests collective coordination may emerge from rapid, local interactions between individuals, pointing to the existence of unidentified decentralized mechanisms for online coordination. The authors aim to uncover these low-order mechanisms.

Method: The authors combine electrophysiology, calcium imaging, optogenetics, and behavioral analysis to characterize a spinal sensorimotor circuit. They also validate findings using a neuromechanical model and a physical robot to test whether a single feedback loop is sufficient to generate vortex phase matching and energetic benefits.

Result: The intraspinal proprioceptive neurons encode both egocentric (self-generated) and allocentric (neighbor-induced) body bending signals, and deliver curvature-based inhibition to precisely time the locomotor network. This enables vortex phase matching, reduces swimming energy costs, and disrupting the circuit uncouples neighboring fish and abolishes schooling.

Conclusion: A spinal circuit dynamically synchronizes individuals through simple local interactions, demonstrating that low-order sensorimotor mechanisms can drive the emergence of coordinated group behavior. This challenges the assumption that collective coordination requires high-order brain processing.

Abstract: The coordinated movement of animal groups is one of the most widespread social behaviors, which are generally attributed to high-order cognitive processing in the brain. Yet, collective coordination can seemingly emerge from rapid, local interactions between individuals, suggesting the existence of decentralized mechanisms of online coordination that remain to be identified. Here, we show that a low-order spinal sensorimotor circuit is required for real-time social coordination during schooling in zebrafish. Central to this circuit are intraspinal proprioceptive neurons that detect local body bending and deliver direct, curvature-based inhibition to precisely time the locomotor network. Combining electrophysiology, calcium imaging, optogenetics, and behavioral analysis, we show that this circuit encodes both self-generated (egocentric) and neighbor-induced (allocentric) body bending signals, enabling fish to match the phase of their swimming to the wakes of their neighbors (vortex phase matching). In a neuromechanical model and physical robot, this single feedback loop is sufficient to generate vortex phase matching and to lower the energetic cost of swimming. Disrupting this circuit uncouples neighboring fish and abolishes schooling behavior. These results show that a spinal circuit dynamically synchronizes individuals through simple, local interactions, revealing how low-order mechanisms can drive the emergence of coordinated group behavior.

### [3] [Beyond Tokens: Probing Higher-Order Epistasis in Learned Protein Representations](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.24953)
*Maryam Rahimimovassagh,Ivan Garibay,Niloofar Yousefi*

Main category: q-bio.QM

TL;DR: ORBIT is a benchmarking framework that separates interaction presence, representation accessibility, and functional recovery in protein fitness landscapes. Applied to the GB1 fitness landscape, it shows that while architectures perform similarly on standard prediction metrics, RIT specifically increases pairwise interaction accessibility at the token stage, while deeper networks improve higher-order recovery through downstream nonlinear capacity.


<details>
  <summary>Details</summary>
Motivation: Protein fitness landscapes contain nonlinear interactions where mutation effects depend on other residues, but conventional prediction metrics may hide representation-level differences in how models encode these interactions.

Method: ORBIT uses Walsh-based diagnostics validated on synthetic landscapes with known interaction order, then analyzes the experimentally measured GB1 fitness landscape under the FLIP 2-vs-rest setting, comparing ridge regression, standard MLP, independent tokens, nonlinear independent tokens, and Residual Interaction Tokenization (RIT) across 20 paired training seeds with pre-specified depth/capacity analyses.

Result: No significant architecture differences in FLIP test R^2 or higher-order functional recovery, but RIT significantly increased pairwise accessibility at the token stage (Delta A_tok,2 = 0.2468, d_z = 1.67, p = 1.14 x 10^-5). Deeper MLPs improved prediction, third-order functional recovery, and accessibility, though fourth-order accessibility remained below zero.

Conclusion: ORBIT reveals representation-level changes hidden by conventional prediction metrics, distinguishing early interaction-aware encoding from higher-order structure constructed by downstream nonlinear capacity.

Abstract: Protein fitness landscapes contain nonlinear interactions in which mutation effects depend on other residues. We introduce ORBIT, an Order-Resolved Benchmarking of Interaction Transformations framework that separates interaction presence, representation accessibility, and functional recovery. ORBIT first validates Walsh-based diagnostics on synthetic landscapes with known interaction order, then analyzes the experimentally measured GB1 fitness landscape under the FLIP 2-vs-rest setting. We compare ridge regression, a standard MLP, independent tokens, nonlinear independent tokens, and Residual Interaction Tokenization (RIT).
  Across 20 paired training seeds, the primary two-hidden-layer comparison found no significant architecture differences in FLIP test R^2, third- or fourth-order functional recovery, or final-layer third- or fourth-order accessibility. However, RIT significantly increased pairwise accessibility at the token stage relative to both independent-token controls (Delta A_tok,2 = 0.2468, d_z = 1.67, Holm-adjusted p = 1.14 x 10^-5), without a detectable downstream higher-order advantage.
  A pre-specified depth/capacity analysis showed that deeper MLPs improved FLIP prediction, third-order functional recovery, and final-layer third-order accessibility; fourth-order accessibility also improved relative to the shallow MLP but remained below zero in absolute held-out R^2. ORBIT therefore reveals representation-level changes hidden by conventional prediction metrics and distinguishes early interaction-aware encoding from higher-order structure constructed by downstream nonlinear capacity.

### [4] [Propensity Straight-Through Gradients for Discrete Stochastic Systems](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.25631)
*Jose M. G. Vilar,Leonor Saiz*

Main category: q-bio.QM

TL;DR: This paper introduces the propensity straight-through (PST) estimator, a gradient estimation method for continuous-time Markov chains (CTMCs) that differentiates exact conditional-mean sensitivities rather than relaxed samples, enabling scalable gradient-based learning through exact stochastic trajectories without temperature or Gumbel noise.


<details>
  <summary>Details</summary>
Motivation: CTMCs are fundamental for modeling discrete stochastic dynamics across many scientific fields, but integrating them with gradient-based machine learning is limited by the hard categorical event selection in Gillespie-type simulation algorithms, which blocks differentiable pathways.

Method: The authors exploit the affine state update to obtain exact one-step conditional-mean sensitivity by differentiating normalized reaction propensities, combining this backward rule with exact forward trajectories to define the PST estimator. They derive the per-step discrepancy in closed form and prove it vanishes for affine downstream dependence.

Result: PST matches Gumbel-Softmax straight-through accuracy across benchmarks (reversible dimerization 0.06% error, genetic oscillator 1.7%, repressilator suite 0.17% median, patch-clamp ion channels R²=0.988), converges 3.0x faster on the oscillator and 2.1x faster on ion channels, and trains a 203,796-parameter stochastic reaction network reaching 98.22% MNIST accuracy.

Conclusion: By differentiating an exact conditional mean rather than a relaxed sample, PST offers a temperature- and Gumbel-free path to scalable gradient-based learning through exact stochastic trajectories, providing both accuracy and computational efficiency advantages.

Abstract: Continuous-time Markov chains (CTMCs) provide the backbone for modeling discrete stochastic dynamics across applied, physical, and biological sciences. Their integration with modern gradient-based machine learning, however, is limited by the hard categorical event selection intrinsic to Gillespie-type simulation algorithms. We exploit the affine state update to obtain the exact one-step conditional-mean sensitivity by differentiating normalized reaction propensities. We pair this backward rule with exact forward trajectories to define the propensity straight-through (PST) estimator. At the trajectory level, we show that one-step sensitivities composed across events can depart from the exact multistep sensitivity. We derive the resulting per-step discrepancy in closed form and prove that it vanishes identically for affine downstream dependence. PST matches the accuracy of Gumbel-Softmax straight-through across all benchmarks: reversible dimerization (0.06% error), a genetic oscillator (1.7% error), a 50-task repressilator suite (0.17% median error), and patch-clamp ion-channel recordings ($R^2$ = 0.988). Under matched settings, PST converges 3.0-fold faster on the oscillator and 2.1-fold faster on the ion channel. At deep-learning scale, PST trains a 203,796-parameter stochastic reaction network with hard sampling, reaching 98.22% MNIST digit classification accuracy. By differentiating an exact conditional mean rather than a relaxed sample, PST offers a temperature- and Gumbel-free path to scalable gradient-based learning through exact stochastic trajectories.

It achieves 97% accuracy on a 45-class subset and 75% accuracy on the full Tomotwin-100 dataset, plus demonstrates recovery of ribosomal assembly states from an unfiltered experimental dataset.


<details>
  <summary>Details</summary>
Motivation: Standard cryo-EM workflows rely on ad hoc iterative classification and filtering strategies to handle impure, heterogeneous samples, which are not systematic or scalable. There is a need for a formalized approach that can handle datasets containing dozens of distinct species for ab initio reconstruction.

Method: The approach formalizes standard practices of iterative classification and filtering into a systematic framework for spawning (launching) and aggregating (merging) multi-class reconstruction jobs across heterogeneous cryo-EM datasets.

Result: The method achieves 97% accuracy on a 45-class subset of Tomotwin-100, 75% accuracy on the full Tomotwin-100 dataset, and successfully recovers ribosomal assembly states from an unfiltered experimental cryo-EM dataset.

Conclusion: This approach's capability scales with computational resources and provides a foundation for automated cryo-EM workflows in modern experimental settings, representing the first method capable of ab initio reconstruction on datasets containing dozens of distinct species.

Abstract: We describe a systematic approach for spawning and aggregating multi-class cryo-EM reconstruction jobs. This approach formalizes standard ad hoc strategies of iterative classification and filtering typically used by practitioners to sort impure, heterogeneous samples. We obtain 97% accuracy on ab initio reconstruction of a 45-class subset of Tomotwin-100, 75% accuracy on the full Tomotwin-100 dataset, and demonstrate recovery of ribosomal assembly states from an unfiltered experimental cryo-EM dataset. ### [6] [What sets the critical genome length for sympatric speciation? A closed form and asymptotic theory](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.25995)
*Dan Braha,Marcus A. M. de Aguiar,Vitor M. Marquioni*

Main category: q-bio.PE

TL;DR: The paper derives an explicit closed-form expression for the critical genome length L_c in the Derrida-Higgs sympatric speciation model, correcting previous moment equations and showing that L_c is determined by competition between deterministic separation from the unrestricted equilibrium and transient genealogical variance. The formula enables asymptotic analysis revealing distinct scaling regimes (μ^-2, M^3/2, or √M/μ) depending on parameter dominance, all confirmed by simulations.


<details>
  <summary>Details</summary>
Motivation: Predicting the critical genome length L_c at which a sexually reproducing population fragments into reproductively isolated species in the Derrida-Higgs model. Prior approaches required numerical iteration of coupled moment equations, and there was an implicit assumption error in the unrestricted moment equations that needed correction.

Method: The authors correct the unrestricted moment equations by removing a previously implicit assumption, then derive an explicit closed-form expression for L_c. The expression captures the competition between deterministic separation from the unrestricted equilibrium and transient genealogical variance at the time the mean overlap reaches q_min. This enables systematic asymptotic analysis across different parameter regimes.

Result: The closed-form expression shows L_c varies by regime: when deterministic contribution dominates, L_c is essentially independent of population size M and scales as μ^-2; when transient genealogical variance dominates, L_c scales as M^3/2 or √M/μ depending on how M and μ jointly vary. Simulations confirm all predicted scaling behaviors.

Conclusion: Transient genealogical variance is identified as the principal mechanism linking finite genome fluctuations to the onset of reproductive fragmentation. The work provides a practical analytical prediction for L_c and corrects the underlying moment equations of the model.

Abstract: In the Derrida--Higgs model of sympatric speciation, a sexually reproducing population with finite binary genomes can mate only when the genetic overlap between two individuals exceeds a threshold $q_{\min}$. Depending on the genome length $L$, the population either remains genetically connected or fragments into reproductively isolated species. A central problem is therefore to predict the critical genome length $L_c$ at which fragmentation begins. At finite $L$, fluctuations broaden the overlap distribution and allow genetically distant regions of the population to remain connected. A previously proposed transient variance criterion captures this effect, but its evaluation requires numerical iteration of coupled moment equations. Here we first correct the unrestricted moment equations by removing a previously implicit assumption and then derive an explicit closed form expression for $L_c$. The resulting formula shows that the critical genome length is determined, at the time the mean overlap reaches $q_{\min}$, by the competition between deterministic separation from the unrestricted equilibrium and the transient genealogical variance of the overlap distribution. This expression permits a systematic asymptotic analysis. When the deterministic contribution dominates, $L_c$ becomes essentially independent of the population size $M$ and scales as $μ^{-2}$. When the transient genealogical variance dominates, $L_c$ grows as $M^{3/2}$ or as $\sqrt{M}/μ$, depending on how $M$ and the mutation rate $μ$ jointly vary. Simulations support all predicted behaviors. Our results identify transient genealogical variance as the principal mechanism linking finite genome fluctuations to the onset of reproductive fragmentation and provide a practical analytical prediction for the critical genome length.

</details>
