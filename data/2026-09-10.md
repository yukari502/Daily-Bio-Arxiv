### [1] [Thermodynamic and Statistical Signatures of Modality Changes in Concentration Distributions Driven by Stochastic Switching Between Two Activity States](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.09979)
*Aindrila Deb,Pintu Patra*

Main category: q-bio.MN

TL;DR: This paper develops a generalized two-state promoter switching model for mRNA accumulation, deriving exact steady-state distributions and analytically computing mean concentration, Fano factor, and entropy production rate (EPR) to reveal how kinetic parameters shape molecular distributions, noise, and dissipation.


<details>
  <summary>Details</summary>
Motivation: Stochastic switching between gene expression states combined with production/degradation dynamics governs mRNA/protein accumulation and phenotype distributions. While the two-state promoter switching model is well-established and quantified via Fano factor and EPR, how these measures correlate with concentration distributions and shift under varying kinetic parameters remains largely unexplored.

Method: The authors use chemical master equations to study a generalized mRNA accumulation model with stochastic switching between two activity states and state-dependent production and degradation rates. They derive exact steady-state probability distributions and analytically compute mean concentration, Fano factor, and EPR, then simplify expressions to isolate contributions from switching rates and relaxation dynamics.

Result: They characterize how Fano factor and EPR vary with mean expression across modality changes driven by switching rates, and identify kinetic parameter conditions that maximize Fano factor and EPR.

Conclusion: The work establishes a generalized framework for stochastic accumulation dynamics, clarifying how kinetic parameters dictate molecular distributions, noise, and dissipation. It extends to protein burst dynamics, phenotype-switching-mediated drug intake, and queuing theory.

Abstract: Stochastic switching between gene expression states, coupled with production and degradation dynamics, governs the accumulation of mRNA and proteins in cells. The concentrations of these accumulated entities dictate the phenotypic distribution of genetically identical cells. The underlying accumulation dynamics are well-captured by a two-state promoter switching model, with statistical and thermodynamic properties quantified via the Fano factor and entropy production rates. However, how these measures correlate with concentration distributions and their shifts under varying kinetic parameters remains largely unexplored. To this end, we use chemical master equations to study a generalized model of mRNA accumulation dynamics in the presence of stochastic switching between two activity states and state-dependent production and degradation rates. We derive exact expressions for the steady-state probability distribution and analytically compute the mean concentration, Fano factor, and entropy production rate (EPR). Simplifying these expressions, we identify contributions arising from stochastic switching rates and relaxation dynamics toward equilibrium in each activity state. Next, using our theoretical results, we characterize the variation in the Fano factor and EPR as a function of mean expression during modality changes of the distributions mediated by the variation of switching rates. We also identify the conditions in kinetic parameters that achieve the highest Fano factor and entropy production rates. Our findings establish a generalized framework for examining stochastic accumulation dynamics, clarifying how kinetic parameters dictate molecular distributions, noise, and dissipation. These insights extend readily to broader contexts coupling stochastic switching with accumulation, including protein burst dynamics, phenotype-switching-mediated drug intake, and queuing theory.

### [2] [Are You Learning Biological Signal or Shortcuts? Auditing and Mitigating Bias in Protein-Protein Interaction Datasets](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.10193)
*Judith Bernett,Anton Spannagl,Joel Ås,Markus List,David B. Blumenthal*

Main category: q-bio.MN

TL;DR: PPI datasets contain study and technical biases that machine learning models exploit as shortcuts. This work systematically characterizes known and previously unreported biases across HIPPIE, IntAct, STRING, and two PDB-derived datasets, and provides an open Nextflow pipeline using integer linear programs for similarity-aware splitting and bias-minimizing negative sampling.


<details>
  <summary>Details</summary>
Motivation: PPI databases do not faithfully reflect biological reality; they are distorted by study and technical biases. Machine learning models can exploit these biases as shortcuts if negative datasets are not carefully constructed. Prior work examined these shortcuts only in isolation.

Method: Systematically characterize reported and previously unreported biases in PPI datasets from HIPPIE, IntAct, STRING, and two PDB 3D-structure-derived datasets. Develop an open Nextflow pipeline combining similarity-aware, data-loss-minimizing dataset splitting and bias-minimizing negative sampling, both formulated as integer linear programs.

Result: Random data splitting introduces strong topological shortcuts. After removing train-test protein overlap, datasets still retain usable shortcuts from self-interactions, taxonomic identity, and functional relatedness, whose prevalence depends on the data source. Sampling negatives from high-confidence non-interactors can amplify the functional-relatedness shortcut.

Conclusion: The proposed pipeline detects and mitigates these biases. The key concept of quantifying biases to minimize them through optimization-based negative sampling can extend to any machine learning problem where negative candidates greatly outnumber positives, beyond PPI prediction.

Abstract: Protein-protein interaction (PPI) databases do not faithfully reflect biological realities. Instead, they are influenced by study and technical biases that distort certain protein and interaction attributes. Machine learning models can exploit these as learning shortcuts if the negative dataset is not constructed with care. So far, the shortcuts introduced during PPI dataset construction have only been examined in isolation. Here, we systematically characterize both reported and, to our knowledge, previously unreported biases in PPI datasets that lead machine learning models to learn shortcuts instead of biological signal. We analyze HIPPIE, IntAct, and STRING, dedicated PPI databases, as well as two datasets derived from 3D-structural information in the Protein Data Bank (PDB). We show that random data splitting introduces strong topological shortcuts. When train-test protein overlap is removed, the resulting datasets still retain usable shortcuts stemming from self-interactions, taxonomic identity, and functional relatedness, whose prevalence interestingly depends on the data source. We further show that sampling negatives from a set of high-confidence non-interactors, an intuitively appealing choice, can amplify the shortcut stemming from functional relatedness. To detect and mitigate these biases, we provide an open Nextflow pipeline that combines similarity-aware, data-loss-minimizing dataset splitting with bias-minimizing negative sampling, both formulated as integer linear programs. Its key concept of quantifying biases to minimize them through optimization-based negative sampling can, in principle, be extended to any machine learning problem where the pool of negative candidates is much larger than the positives and is thus of interest also beyond PPI prediction.

### [3] [Revisiting the Aerts-Broekaert-Smets quantum model of the liar paradox](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.09228)
*Massimiliano Sassoli de Bianchi*

Main category: q-bio.NC

TL;DR: This paper reconstructs the two-sentence liar paradox quantum model of Aerts, Broekaert, and Smets in Dirac notation, clarifying its structure and offering new observations on entanglement, Hamiltonian dynamics, and the role of state-space enlargement.


<details>
  <summary>Details</summary>
Motivation: The original quantum model of the two-sentence liar paradox is an early example of quantum formalism applied to cognitive dynamics, but its structure remains opaque in places. The authors aim to clarify the model, distinguish truth values from decision versus semantic inference, and understand why the state-space enlargement is needed.

Method: The model is rewritten in Dirac notation. The authors distinguish truth values arising from decision versus semantic inference, analyze the associated factorization of the one-sentence state space, derive the Hamiltonian generating the four-state cyclic evolution, express it in compact operator form, and compute transition probabilities in closed form. They also examine a unitarily equivalent representation in the four-dimensional truth-only space and consider the effect of admitting revision processes.

Result: The enlargement of the state space corresponds to a factorization where non-paradoxical configurations are separable but the genuine liar state is entangled. The four-state cyclic evolution has a Hamiltonian expressible in compact operator form with closed-form transition probabilities. The liar cycle has a unitarily equivalent representation in the four-dimensional truth-only space where the unmeasured liar state is separable, so the enlargement is not required by the unitary dynamics alone—it is required by the measurement structure, and also by the dynamics once revision processes are allowed.

Conclusion: The dimensional enlargement is not demanded by the unitary part of the dynamics but is required by the measurement structure and by revision processes, where states with identical truth content but different origins have different successors, making the origin degree of freedom a minimal form of cognitive memory. The authors discuss possible links between the model and more general deliberative processes.

Abstract: The quantum model of the two-sentence liar paradox proposed by Aerts, Broekaert, and Smets is an early example of the use of quantum formalism to describe cognitive dynamics. Our reconstruction is primarily pedagogical in intent, but it also leads to a number of clarifications, and to some new observations, concerning the structure of the model. Rewriting the model in Dirac notation, we make explicit the distinction between truth values originating from a decision and from semantic inference, and show that the associated enlargement of the one-sentence state space corresponds to a factorization with respect to which the non-paradoxical configurations are separable while the genuine liar state is entangled. We also derive the Hamiltonian generating the four-state cyclic evolution, express it in compact operator form, and give the resulting transition probabilities in closed form. We emphasize that the liar cycle admits a unitarily equivalent representation in the four-dimensional truth-only space, where the unmeasured liar state is separable, so the dimensional enlargement is not required by the unitary part of the dynamics; it is required, however, by the measurement structure. The enlargement is also required by the dynamics as soon as revision processes are admitted, in which states with identical truth content but different origins have different successors, the origin degree of freedom then acting as a minimal form of cognitive memory. We conclude by discussing the possible relationship between the model and more general deliberative processes.

### [4] [Persistence of n-Species Lotka-Volterra Models with Periodic Pulses](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.09343)
*Eleanor Courcelle,Jane Shaw MacDonald,Swati Patel*

Main category: q-bio.PE

TL;DR: This paper derives general sufficient conditions for permanence (and robust permanence) in n-species population models under periodic multiplicative pulse disturbances, and illustrates them with two Lotka-Volterra biological control examples.


<details>
  <summary>Details</summary>
Motivation: Periodic impulsive interventions (chemotherapy, pesticide application, infectious-disease treatment) are common in population management, but classical permanence theory does not directly handle models with discrete periodic pulses, so a general framework is needed.

Method: The impulsive system is transformed into an associated autonomous continuous-time dynamical system, allowing classical permanence theory (via Morse decomposition of the extinction set and weighted long-term growth rates) to be extended; conditions separate the roles of continuous dynamics and periodic pulses, and robustness under small perturbations is also analyzed.

Result: A sufficient condition for permanence is given in terms of weighted long-term growth rates on the extinction set's Morse decomposition; the same conditions yield robust permanence under small perturbations to dynamics, pulse period, and pulse effects. Two Lotka-Volterra examples (chemo-sensitive vs. resistant cancer cells; pesticide-plus-parasitoid pest control) show how intervention frequency and intensity interact with ecological interactions to determine coexistence vs. exclusion.

Conclusion: The paper provides a general framework for analyzing persistence in ecological systems subject to repeated discrete disturbances.

Abstract: Periodic impulsive interventions arise naturally in the management of biological populations, including chemotherapy, pesticide application, and infectious-disease treatment. We develop general conditions for permanence in n-species population models subject to periodic multiplicative pulse disturbances. Our main result provides a sufficient condition for permanence in terms of weighted long-term growth rates on a Morse decomposition of the extinction set, explicitly separating the contributions of continuous population dynamics from those of the periodic pulse. To establish this result, we transform the impulsive system into an associated autonomous continuous-time dynamical system and use this correspondence to extend classical permanence theory to periodically pulsed models. We further show that the same conditions imply robust permanence under sufficiently small perturbations to the continuous dynamics, pulse period, and pulse effects. We illustrate the framework with two Lotka-Volterra models motivated by biological control: competition between chemotherapy-sensitive and chemotherapy-resistant cancer cells, and integrated control of an agricultural pest using pesticides and parasitoids. These examples demonstrate how intervention frequency and intensity interact with underlying ecological interactions to determine whether populations coexist or are excluded. Our results provide a general framework for analyzing persistence in ecological systems subject to repeated discrete disturbances.

### [5] [Population Ecology of Tunes](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.09501)
*John M. McBride,Armand. M. Leroi*

Main category: q-bio.PE

TL;DR: Using 13 years of weekly popularity data for ~20,000 Irish traditional tunes, the authors fit ecological birth-process models to show that tunes differ in intrinsic fitness, yet repertoire diversity still increases due to continual arrival of new compositions.


<details>
  <summary>Details</summary>
Motivation: How cultural repertoires maintain diversity under selection is a fundamental question in cultural evolution. Irish traditional music offers a large, well-documented dataset to test whether selection and diversity can coexist.

Method: Fit ecological birth-process models under neutral, frequency-dependent, and per-tune selection hypotheses to weekly popularity data for ~20,000 Irish traditional tunes over thirteen years. Analyze fitness variance explained by social and melodic features, linkage via tune sets, and fitness changes over time compared with recordings.

Result: Strong evidence that tunes differ in intrinsic fitness. 29% of fitness variance is explained by a mixture of social and melodic features. Some tunes are carried along via linkage from the tradition of playing tunes in sets (analogous to selective sweeps). The mechanism by which a long-dormant tune becomes fit through a popular recording is precisely identified. Repertoire diversity nevertheless increases due to continual arrival of new compositions.

Conclusion: Selection and diversity can coexist in a cultural ecosystem. Irish traditional music is established as a quantitatively tractable system for studying cultural variant evolution and understanding what makes a tune stand out.

Abstract: How cultural repertoires maintain diversity under selection is a fundamental question in cultural evolution. We address this using thirteen years of weekly popularity data for approximately 20,000 Irish traditional tunes, fitting ecological birth-process models under neutral, frequency-dependent, and per-tune selection hypotheses. We find strong evidence that tunes differ in intrinsic fitness - some are systematically more likely to be learned than others. We find that 29% of the variance in fitness can be explained by a mixture of social and melodic features. Some tunes appear to be carried along via linkage due to the tradition of playing tunes in sets, analogous to selective sweeps in genetics. By measuring changes in fitness over time and comparing this with recordings we precisely identify the mechanism by which a long-dormant tune can become fit through a popular recording. Despite the directional selection, repertoire diversity increases, driven by the continual arrival of new compositions. These results demonstrate that selection and diversity can coexist in a cultural ecosystem, and establish Irish traditional music as a quantitatively tractable system for studying the evolution of cultural variants and understanding what makes a tune stand out.

Main category: q-bio.QM

TL;DR: A YOLOv8-based multi-task framework automates bacterial colony counting and species classification on the AGAR dataset, achieving ~98% accuracy, then is optimized via pruning and quantized ONNX inference for Raspberry Pi deployment.


<details>
  <summary>Details</summary>
Motivation: Manual counting and classification of bacterial colonies are labor-intensive and error-prone, especially on densely populated plates, motivating automated deep learning solutions that can run in resource-constrained laboratory settings.

Method: Trained on the AGAR dataset (18,000 images; 9,202 train / 3,067 test). A custom multi-task CNN with global regression served as baseline but failed in clustered colonies due to lack of spatial localization. Replaced with YOLOv8 object detection at 1024x1024 resolution for instance-level detection and label assignment. Deployment optimization via unstructured and structured pruning, ONNX conversion, and reduced-precision inference (FP32, FP16, INT8).

Result: Classification accuracy 98.13%, counting accuracy 98.27% (within 10-colony margin). On Raspberry Pi 4B, ONNX FP32/FP16 gave best trade-off (~6.4s inference, MAE ~2.20). Unstructured pruning preserved accuracy (MAE ~2.01) with no runtime gains; structured pruning degraded accuracy significantly (MAE ~6.3).

Conclusion: YOLOv8-based instance-level detection is highly effective for colony counting and classification, but instance-level detection is sensitive to architectural compression. ONNX FP32/FP16 quantization offers the best practical balance for edge deployment, providing guidance for optimization strategy selection in resource-constrained labs.

Abstract: Manual counting and classification of bacterial colonies are critical yet labor-intensive tasks in microbiology, prone to human error particularly on densely populated plates. This work proposes a multi-task deep learning framework trained on the Annotated Germs for Automated Recognition (AGAR) dataset (18,000 images; 9,202 training / 3,067 testing) to automate Colony Forming Unit (CFU) enumeration and species classification. The model achieved a classification accuracy of 98.13% and a counting accuracy of 98.27% (within a 10-colony margin), demonstrating strong predictive capability. On a Raspberry Pi 4B, ONNX FP32 and FP16 variants offered the best balance between inference speed (~6.4s) and accuracy (MAE ~2.20). Unstructured pruning preserved predictive accuracy (MAE ~2.01) without runtime gains, while structured pruning resulted in significant accuracy degradation (MAE ~6.3), revealing the sensitivity of instance-level colony detection to architectural compression. These findings provide practical guidance for selecting optimization strategies in resource-constrained laboratory deployments.
