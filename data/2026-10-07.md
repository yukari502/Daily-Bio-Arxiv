### [2] [Neural networks as decision trees: an analytical solution for learning and neural selectivity](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.08228)
*Hugo Tissot,Jonas Ranft,Yves Boubenec*

Main category: q-bio.NC

TL;DR: The paper presents an analytical framework showing that piecewise-linear networks decompose tasks into local linear regressions over activation regions, yielding a decision-tree interpretation and predicting neural selectivity geometry.


<details>
  <summary>Details</summary>
Motivation: Nonlinear neural networks develop structured internal representations, but how their geometry is determined by the tasks being learned remains poorly understood.

Method: Develops an analytical framework for piecewise-linear feedforward and recurrent networks linking learning, activation-region structure, and neural selectivity. Shows that at stationary points of gradient-aligned learning, networks decompose into local linear regressions over activation regions; deviations from least-squares solutions are constrained by shared weights and vanish in low-error regimes. Derives a decision-tree interpretation (recovered numerically) and analyzes how active regions determine captured task statistics.

Result: Predictions closely match selectivity geometry in simulated networks and two empirical neural datasets. Neural baseline is shown to regulate activation pattern diversity, placing networks along a continuum between coarse, generalizing and fine-grained, expressive representations.

Conclusion: Activation regions provide a unifying framework for describing how nonlinear networks decompose task structure into local computations and how those computations can be revealed through neural activity.

Abstract: Nonlinear neural networks develop structured internal representations, yet how their geometry is determined by the tasks being learned remains poorly understood. Here, we develop an analytical framework for piecewise-linear feedforward and recurrent networks that links learning, activation-region structure, and neural selectivity. We show that, at any stationary point of gradient-aligned learning, a nonlinear network decomposes into local linear regressions over its activation regions. Deviations from the corresponding least-squares solutions are jointly constrained by network weights shared across regions and vanish in the low-error regime, yielding an approximate piecewise least-squares decomposition of the task. This structure admits a decision-tree interpretation, which we recover numerically by fitting trees to predict network activation patterns from the input. We further derive how the regions in which neurons are active determine the task statistics they capture, thereby organizing neural selectivity into distinct subpopulations. The resulting predictions closely match the selectivity geometry observed in simulated networks and two empirical neural datasets. Finally, we show that neural baseline regulates activation pattern diversity, placing networks along a continuum between coarse, generalizing representations and fine-grained, expressive representations. Together, these results establish activation regions as a unifying framework for describing how nonlinear networks decompose task structure into local computations and how those computations can be revealed through neural activity.

### [9] [Effects of Periodic Migration on Selection in Subdivided Populations](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.08160)

Main category: q-bio.PE

TL;DR: Periodic (rather than constant) migration in a two-deme Moran model with heterogeneous selection drives mutant frequency toward neutrality, with the effect maximized for out-of-phase and directionally asymmetric migration and showing a non-monotonic dependence on migration period.


<details>
  <summary>Details</summary>
Motivation: Migration and selection jointly shape evolution in spatially structured populations, and migration rates fluctuate over time due to ecological shifts (e.g., sea-level changes altering habitat connectivity). Prior work largely assumes constant migration, leaving unclear how the temporal organization of gene flow—holding the time-averaged rate fixed—affects selection outcomes.

Method: A two-deme Moran model with spatially heterogeneous selection is used, where a mutant is advantageous in one habitat and disadvantageous in the other. The response to period is non-monotonic, with an intermediate characteristic period yielding the largest deviation under in-phase migration.

Conclusion: The effects arise from coupling between migration and local selection in the Moran process: temporal modulation changes both individual exchange between habitats and the local population composition on which selection acts. Abstract: Migration and selection jointly shape evolutionary outcomes in spatially structured populations.
