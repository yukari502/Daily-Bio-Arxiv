Method: The authors propose an 'axis-resolved' measurement. They validate this by comparing the shift against null models (permutation tests) to ensure the signal is not a byproduct of nuisance variables.

Result: The method was applied to two distinct systems with highly successful results: 
1. **Biological (Mouse V1):** It successfully identified the structural signal related to orientation coding and confirmed the $O(2)$ symmetry of the cortical code, distinguishing between even and odd symmetry sectors.
2. **Artificial (LLMs):** It separated token-inherited content from depth-constructed syntax across five architectures, revealing that grammaticality signals are a result of 'carrier composition' rather than just noise.

Abstract: How much of a neural population's measured dimensionality is the code, and how much is the act of measuring it? The slope of the participation ratio against subsample size is reported for cortical populations and for the layers of language models as a number that belongs to the system. It does not. One patch of eleven thousand mouse V1 neurons returns three slopes, 0.25 along random stimulus subsets, 0.31 along drift direction, 0.35 along neuron count, and only the middle one carries structure; the other two are the sampling floor. We split every exponent into that floor and a shift earned along a declared axis, and read the shift against two nulls: permuting the labels, and permuting them only among samples that share a nuisance. On V1 the direction-aligned shift clears the label null in eight of eight grating recordings, is positive in 32 of 32 animals in a second laboratory, and reads both sectors of the code's O(2) symmetry. On language models the same split separates content inherited from the tokens from construction built with depth, on five architectures, and the second null shows that a grammaticality signal of twenty standard deviations is entirely carrier composition. With a floor and a declared axis, dimensionality scaling is an axis-resolved measurement, and where the axis follows a known symmetry it resolves the sector content of the code. Abstract: Genomic offset methods quantify the genetic consequences of environmental change using genotype environment associations (GEA), but the conditions under which they predict fitness remain poorly understood. However, this relationship does not necessarily hold for a realized genetic architecture. We identify two complementary mechanisms that can explain high predictive accuracy of genomic offset statistics. First, when effective adaptive dimensionality is sufficiently large, the genetically mediated fitness response concentrates around its marginal expectation, reducing the dependence on the particular genetic architecture. Second, even with few adaptive traits, accurate predictions can arise from geometric alignment between the complete fitness loss matrix and the GEA metric.
